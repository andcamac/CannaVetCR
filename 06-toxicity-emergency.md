# THC Toxicosis — Recognition & Emergency Support (Dogs & Cats)

This is distinct from therapeutic CBD use: this section covers accidental/recreational-marijuana ingestion, which is a genuine emergency-medicine topic, not a dosing topic. **An agent should treat any query matching this pattern as "recommend immediate veterinary evaluation," not as a self-treatment guide.**

## Why dogs/cats are more sensitive than humans

Dogs in particular have a much higher density of cannabinoid (CB1) receptors in the brain than humans, which is why doses that wouldn't meaningfully affect a person can cause marked intoxication in a dog.

## Onset and clinical course

- Clinical signs typically appear **30–60 minutes** after ingestion.
- Classic presentation: depression/lethargy, ataxia (wobbliness), and dribbling urine (urinary incontinence) — described in the veterinary literature as the "classic triad."
- Other reported signs: increased sensitivity to sound/light/touch, dilated pupils, slow heart rate, loss of balance, vomiting, tremors, and (in severe cases) agitation or seizures.
- Effects typically last **18–24 hours** in dogs, with full recovery usually within 24–36 hours, though some cases take up to 72 hours depending on dose. One case-series source cites a recovery window as broad as 30 minutes to 4 days.
- **Death is extremely rare.** The Merck Veterinary Manual states signs are self-limiting within 1–3 days and death is extremely rare from THC alone. Two case reports of fatalities involve concentrated-THC butter in baked goods — a very different exposure scale from typical accidental ingestion.
- Synthetic cannabinoids (as opposed to plant-derived THC) carry a comparatively worse (only "fair") prognosis and should be treated with more caution.

## Diagnosis

- Usually based on history + clinical signs; a positive human urine THC drug-screen test can support (not confirm) diagnosis, but false negatives are possible (THC can bind to glass/rubber in some sample containers) and these tests are not validated for veterinary use.
- Many cases are unwitnessed — an animal may show signs after an unsupervised walk/being outside, with no known ingestion history reported by the owner.

## Treatment (supportive care — always requires a veterinarian)

- Decontamination if very recent (within roughly 1–2 hours of ingestion): induced vomiting and/or activated charcoal, weighed against aspiration risk in an already sedated/ataxic patient.
- IV fluids, temperature regulation (warming or cooling as needed), and general nursing/monitoring.
- Medications for nausea/vomiting, agitation, tremors, or seizures as clinically indicated.
- **Intravenous lipid emulsion therapy** has been used successfully in at least one severe case reported in the literature (Culler & Vigani, 2019) as an extracorporeal-style approach to help bind and clear THC in a compromised patient.
- A 2024 case series (Marsigliano et al., *Frontiers in Veterinary Science*) reported that **transmucosal CBD-infused dissolving sheets** led to expedient resolution of clinical signs in six dogs with non-medical THC toxicosis — an emerging, minimally invasive supportive option, notable because it's cheaper/more accessible than full supportive hospitalization, though based on a small case series rather than an RCT.

## Co-ingestion red flags

Edibles frequently contain **chocolate** and/or **xylitol** — both separately toxic to dogs. Any suspected THC edible ingestion should prompt the same urgency as if those co-ingredients were confirmed, since both substantially change the emergency picture and treatment priority.

## Agent behavior for this topic

- Any query describing a pet that may have ingested marijuana/THC product should get: (1) an immediate "contact your vet or nearest emergency clinic / poison control now" instruction, (2) what to watch for en route, (3) never a home-dosing or "wait and see" recommendation, and (4) a prompt to check for chocolate/xylitol co-ingestion if it was an edible.
