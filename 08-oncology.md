# Cannabinoids and Cancer / Oncology

**Read this before using anything in this file with a client.** Almost everything here is preclinical (cell-culture or rodent-model) evidence. It explains *why researchers are interested* in cannabinoids for veterinary oncology — it is not evidence that any cannabis product treats, shrinks, or cures cancer in a live dog or cat. The US FDA has issued formal warning letters to pet-CBD companies specifically over cancer-treatment marketing claims (see `01-legal-regulatory.md`); this section is written carefully to avoid making the same category of claim.

## The one real animal-level trial: safety alongside chemotherapy

**Lejeune et al. 2025**, *J Vet Intern Med* 39(4):e70179 — a prospective, double-blinded, randomized, placebo-controlled trial. 25 client-owned dogs with high-grade lymphoma undergoing one cycle of **CHOP chemotherapy** (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisone) received either **5 mg/kg CBD/CBDA-rich hemp oil every 12 hours** or placebo. 19 dogs completed the trial.

- No significant increase in hematologic or biochemical adverse events vs. placebo.
- CBD/CBDA supplementation did not significantly change doxorubicin exposure (AUC).
- **This is a safety/pharmacokinetic trial, not a tumor-response trial.** It supports that CBD/CBDA can likely be added to a CHOP protocol without disrupting chemo drug levels or adding toxicity. It says nothing about whether CBD affected the lymphoma itself.

[Source (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1111/jvim.70179)

## Preclinical (in vitro / cell-line) findings, by tumor type

All figures below are **cell-culture concentrations (µM or µg/mL) or mouse-model doses** — they cannot be converted into an oral mg/kg dose for a live veterinary patient. They are included so a clinician can see what's driving research interest, not as dosing information.

**Lymphoma**: CBD, THC, and the synthetic agonist WIN 55,212-2 reduced viability of canine B-cell (1771, CLBL-1) and T-cell (CL-1) non-Hodgkin lymphoma cell lines in culture, CBD often most potent ([PMID 38672512](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38672512/)). A related study found canine NHL cells respond to the endocannabinoids AEA and 2-AG, with higher CB1/CB2 expression in B-cell lines ([PMID 37330042](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37330042/)).

**Mammary carcinoma**: Primary canine mammary carcinoma cells over-express CB1, CB2, TRPV1, GPR55, and PPAR-α. CBD at 10–20 µM lowered inflammatory markers (COX-2, IL-6, TNF-α) without killing the cells at those concentrations ([PMID 42078490](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42078490/)). A CBD-loaded nanoemulsion tested in CF41.Mg and IPC366 canine mammary carcinoma cell lines reduced viability, increased apoptosis, and reduced migration/invasion, with less toxicity to normal kidney (MDCK) cells than to the cancer lines ([PMID 40870993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40870993/) / [PMC12389271](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12389271/)).

**Prostate carcinoma**: CBD-rich and THC-rich cannabis extracts reduced viability of canine prostate carcinoma cell lines (PC1/PC2); IC50 roughly 3.4–3.6 µM (CBD-rich) and 4.5–4.9 µM (THC-rich), cell death confirmed by annexin V/PI staining ([PMID 39453093](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39453093/) / [PMC11512242](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11512242/)).

**Urothelial (bladder) carcinoma**: CBD alone reduced viability and induced apoptosis in canine urothelial carcinoma cells, with synergy when combined with mitoxantrone or vinblastine — but not with carboplatin ([PMID 34352013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34352013/) / [PMC8341525](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8341525/)).

**Mixed canine tumor lines**: Across five canine neoplastic cell lines, CBD was far more potent than CBDA at reducing proliferation via autophagy followed by apoptosis, with synergistic/additive effects alongside vincristine and mixed results alongside doxorubicin ([PMID 33247539](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33247539/)).

**Glioma**: CBD induced apoptosis and disrupted mitochondrial function (via the VDAC1 channel) in both canine and human glioma cell lines in culture — canine cells were more sensitive in that assay, with cytotoxicity in the 4.9–8.2 µg/mL range (Gross et al. 2021, *Front Pharmacol* 12:725136, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2021.725136)). A separate study transplanted a canine glioma line (J3TBG) into immunodeficient mice and combined CBD with radiation therapy; treated mice trended toward longer survival than radiation alone, but the difference was not statistically significant in that small study ([PMC12390246](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12390246/)).

**Mast cell tumors (biomarker research, not treatment)**: CB2 receptor staining was higher in lower-grade cutaneous mast cell tumors ([PMID 38130452](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38130452/)). Dogs with cutaneous mast cell tumors had higher circulating 2-AG and lower AEA/PEA than healthy dogs, suggesting 2-AG as a possible biomarker worth further study ([PMID 39457916](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39457916/) / [PMC11503820](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11503820/)).

## Supportive (non-antitumor) finding worth knowing

In female dogs undergoing mastectomy for mammary neoplasia (combined with ovariohysterectomy), **perioperative full-spectrum CBD/THC oil reduced the propofol and sevoflurane needed** for anesthesia. This is an anesthesia-sparing finding, not an antitumor one — but it may be clinically useful for surgical planning ([PMC13303699](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13303699/)).

## Reviews to start with

- della Rocca et al. 2025, *Animals* 15(15):2185 — the endocannabinoid system's role in companion-animal oncology. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani15152185)
- Systematic review, *Frontiers in Veterinary Science* 2026, "Potential antitumor effect of cannabidiol (CBD) in canine oncology" — covers lymphoma, mammary, glioma, prostate, osteosarcoma, urothelial; mostly preclinical. [PMID 42254894](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42254894/)

**Bottom line from this literature**: lab studies repeatedly show CBD (and sometimes THC-rich extracts or endocannabinoids) can reduce viability of several canine cancer cell types in culture, sometimes with chemotherapy synergy. There is not yet strong published evidence that cannabis products shrink or cure cancer in client-owned dogs. The lymphoma CHOP trial above only supports short-term tolerability of CBD/CBDA alongside chemo — not tumor response. Treatment decisions belong with a veterinary oncologist.
