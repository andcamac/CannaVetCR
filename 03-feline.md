# Cannabinoids in Cats

Cat data lags dogs substantially — far fewer trials, and cats metabolize CBD differently (limited hepatic glucuronidation), so canine dosing should **not** be extrapolated directly to cats.

## Key safety/tolerance studies

- Coltherd et al. (2023/2024), *Frontiers in Veterinary Science* — two randomized, blinded, placebo-controlled studies in healthy cats:
  - Study 1: 4 mg/kg/day THC-free CBD distillate vs. placebo, 4 weeks, with plasma CBD time-course sampling at day 0 and day 14.
  - Study 2: same 4 mg/kg/day dose vs. placebo over **26 weeks**, with a comprehensive panel of physiological health measures at baseline, 4, 10, 18, and 26 weeks, plus a 4-week washout. This is the longest-duration published feline CBD safety study to date.
  - Reported as generally well tolerated; a persistent ALT elevation was noted in at least one cat during the associated safety literature, underscoring the value of liver-enzyme monitoring.
- Peak plasma CBD concentration in cats occurs around 2 hours post-dose in the postprandial time-course data; CBD has a shorter half-life in cats (roughly 1.5–2.1 hours at 2 mg/kg) than in dogs, and lower bioavailability overall.
- The human CBD anti-seizure drug Epidiolex®, when tested in fasted vs. fed cats at 5 mg/kg, showed higher plasma concentration and AUC when given with food — suggesting (not yet confirmed for hemp-derived distillates) that feeding alongside dosing may matter for cats too.
- Escalating-dose feline safety work has found single doses up to 80 mg/kg tolerated in short-term testing — but this is a tolerance ceiling from a safety study, **not** a recommended therapeutic dose.

## Practical dosing notes (lower evidence tier)

- Commonly cited starting-dose heuristic: **0.1–0.5 mg/kg**, notably lower per body weight than typical dog starting points, reflecting cats' greater sensitivity and slower/different clearance.
- Some consumer-facing sources claim cats need a *higher* per-pound dose than dogs (~2x); this conflicts with the "start lower, cats are more sensitive" framing from the peer-reviewed safety literature above. **This is a real inconsistency in the public information landscape** — an agent should surface the peer-reviewed 4 mg/kg tolerance-study data as the more authoritative reference point and flag the disagreement rather than average the two framings together.
- Recommended monitoring in the practical literature: baseline ALT and a recheck around 6 weeks into any sustained course.
- Avoid products containing xylitol (a common human-food sweetener toxic to pets) or any measurable THC — cats are also THC-sensitive.

## Evidence gaps specific to cats

- No published feline-specific RCT yet for a clinical condition (e.g., feline osteoarthritis, chronic kidney disease-associated inflammation) analogous to the canine OA/epilepsy trials — current feline literature is overwhelmingly safety/tolerance-focused in healthy animals, not efficacy-focused in diseased populations.
- This is the single biggest gap to flag to a clinic evaluating whether to recommend CBD for a specific feline diagnosis: **there is currently much better evidence that CBD is tolerated in healthy cats than that it is effective for any specific feline disease.**
